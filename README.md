# Hand control training (v0.39)

A one-minute training page for the webcam hand control in the [Planetary Modeling Atlas](https://tbirdpower3000.github.io/planetary-atlas-v15mk2/). It teaches the atlas how *your* hand moves, so the cursor reaches the whole screen, your pinch is read reliably, and buttons are as easy to hit as you need.

**Use it:** https://tbirdpower3000.github.io/hand-control-training/ (Chrome, Edge or Safari, with a webcam).

## What it does

1. Asks which hand you use, how steady it is, how far you sit from the camera, and which cursor and sounds you want.
2. **Reach:** you move your open hand to four comfortable corners, and the page maps that area to the whole screen. After each corner it asks how easy that reach was (1–7), so hard reaches (for example up high) show up per corner.
3. **Pinch:** open hand, then a firm pinch, three times, to set your own pinch threshold.
4. **Hold still:** three seconds on a dot, to measure how much the cursor wobbles and set the smoothing.
5. **Targets:** eight buttons to pinch, to measure overshoots, misses and time, which set how strongly buttons pull the cursor in.
6. **Fast:** dart across the screen to a ring and stop on it; it measures peak speed, how far the smoothed cursor lags behind your hand, overshoot and time to settle, which tune how quickly the cursor follows fast moves.
7. After each step it asks one question: overall, how easy was that, from 1 (very hard, a lot of effort) to 7 (easy, no effort). You pinch a number and confirm it, or let it carry on by itself from what it measured.

**🎈 Pop the bubbles** is an optional 45-second game (on the first card and the summary): bubbles of three sizes (36, 56 and 84 px; small ones score more) fall from the top and you pinch them. The end card shows how many of each size you popped and your precision: the spread of your pops around the bubbles' centres as an effective width (4.133 × the standard deviation, as in pointing studies), the size of target you hit almost every time. It uses the trained settings of the hand you play with, gets faster as you score, slows down after three get away in a row, and pauses while your hand rests below the waist line. Scores go to the private history only. After each round with at least 8 pinches it also fine-tunes the saved settings of the hand that played (pinch thresholds in the image and in 3D, and the pull onto buttons), moving 30% of the way toward what the round measured. Each change is a trial: if the next round with that hand is clearly worse, it is undone by itself, and there is an Undo button. Only trained hands are tuned. On the end card your hand works the buttons, so you can pinch 🎈 Play again without touching the mouse.

**Tricks from game design** (in the game, the rating question and the end card; the measuring steps stay untouched):
- *Speed-based gain*, as on a mouse or console: slow hand movements move the cursor less (down to 45%) for precision, quick ones move it fully; it never strays more than 60 px from your hand.
- *Forgiving presses*: a pinch up to 150 ms after the cursor slips off a target still counts for it, and a pinch up to 150 ms early counts when you arrive with the fingers still closed. Small bubbles have an invisible hit area bigger than they look (40 px from the centre).

**📦 Carry the disc** is a 60-second game of picking up and placing: move onto the disc, pinch and hold to pick it up, carry it into the shaded square and open your hand to drop it. The square visits the four corners and then the middle, then again smaller (170, 130 and 100 px). The end card shows deliveries, slips (the disc dropped outside a square), placement error from the square's centre, time per delivery, path straightness and how long each carry was held; a one-frame flicker of the pinch on the way is held through. After each round with at least 4 deliveries it fine-tunes the saved settings of the hand that played: slips and pinch flickers widen the gap between pinch-on and pinch-off; landing well short of a square's centre weakens the hold on targets, tight landings strengthen it a little; a wandering path adds smoothing and a straight one takes some away; a corner that takes much longer than the middle grows the reach box toward it. Each change is a trial judged by the next round's score, with an Undo button. Results go to the private history only.
- *A press you can feel*: the target bounces and a ring bursts from where you pressed.

**Tuning.** The game's tuning is judged by throughput (speed and precision in one number) over at least 20 pops, so chance variation can't undo a good change or keep a bad one; a change needs 12 pinches to be made.

**Hand models on trial.** There are four ways of turning the tracked hand into the one point the cursor follows: the palm centre (steady, but a palm's length behind the fingertip), the index fingertip itself (exact, but the jumpiest point), a virtual fingertip rebuilt from the palm's direction, and a fitted palm (a five-point palm shape learned for you, fitted to each frame, with the fingertip's usual place in it). The games try them in turn, one per round, shown only as a letter so you can't tell which is which, and the private comparison shows how each did (throughput, placement, slips). Once every model has had two rounds an **adaptive** mode joins the draw: before each target it looks at the task, how tilted and how near the hand is, which hand, how fast it is moving and the target's size, picks the model it expects to do best, and learns from how it went. On the comparison page you can lock one model, pick adaptive, or keep trialling. The pinch is measured the same way whichever model is in use.

**Feature trials.** Seven candidate improvements to the hand control are on trial in the games, each behind a switch: a hold that lets a deliberate move through while still damping jitter (*dispersive hold*), a hold that acts only inside a button (*hold only inside*), a smooth pull back to the hand instead of a step (*dead-zone tether*), a filter that cancels a steady tremor at its own frequency (*tremor notch*), a hand-over to the next button when the hand has clearly moved on (*neighbour transfer*), a pinch read from where all six thumb and index joints are rather than one distance (*pinch by projection*), and a check that the finger bones have plausible lengths, which catches the tracker mixing up landmarks (*bone-chain gate*). Each round of either game switches each one on or off by a balanced draw (on in exactly four of every eight rounds), shown in the game only as a neutral code. The private comparison page then estimates each feature's effect on the pop game's throughput and the carry game's score with a least-squares fit (allowing for which hand model was in use), and says which ones to **implement in the atlas** (a clearly positive effect, t ≥ 2), which to **remove** (a clear loss, or no effect after 12 rounds with it on) and which to keep trialling; it also lists models the adaptive chooser never picks, and how often each feature and model was actually in play. "Copy suggestions" puts that summary on the clipboard. `scripts/unused.py` lists functions in the page that nothing refers to (`UNUSED.md`).

**Camera models on trial.** Three ways of turning the camera picture into a cursor position that account for how far you sit and for leaning are tried at random against today's flat box, one per game round, shown only as a neutral "camera" letter. *Eye-anchored*: the cursor follows where your hand is relative to the point between your eyes, in centimetres (the gap between your pupils tells the page how far away you are), so leaning in or moving your chair doesn't move the cursor. *Eye-anchored with a nine-point fit*: the same, with a gentle curve fitted from the Reach step's holds, used only when it predicts the holds better than the plain box. *Arm angle*: the cursor follows your arm's angle about the shoulder; when the arm is bent more than at calibration it falls back to eye-anchored for that moment. The Reach step now has nine holds (the four corners with their questions, then the middles of the four edges and the middle, without questions) and measures where your eyes are at each. Hands trained before v0.35 play the flat box until they're trained again. The comparison page shows each camera model's throughput, placement at the corners and in the middle, and slips, and a model that clearly beats the flat box is listed under "Suggested for the atlas".

**🎞 Replay** draws what happened in the last few minutes (targets, prompts, the cursor and its trail, presses, and the tracked hand points with the waist line) so a problem can be watched again. You can download the replay log and send it in, or open one. It holds no camera picture, stays in the tab until you reload, and is never sent anywhere by itself.

The profile (a few numbers) is saved in your browser. The atlas, which lives on the same site, picks it up the next time you start hand control, and its 🎯 Adaptive aim keeps fine-tuning it as you go. You can also download the profile as a file and open it in the atlas with "Open a planet pack".

**v0.38.** A carried disc keeps up with your hand: the extra hold that stops a pinch from dragging the cursor now lasts only while the fingers are closing or opening, not for the whole held pinch, so the disc no longer trails 60–170 px behind and creeps the last few centimetres into the square. The precision figure counts your misses too, so it no longer understates the spread of a shaky hand, and the end card now gives the width you land 96% of pops within. A button's highlight no longer flickers when the cursor sits at its edge: it comes on at the edge but goes off only once the cursor is clearly outside, by about your own measured wobble.

## Choosing your aim

The first card is a set of big tiles: **Train**, **Pop the bubbles**, **Carry the disc**, **Choose your aim**, **Compare**, **Share results** and **Back to the atlas**. Once the camera is on, your hand works every card: move onto a tile, button or option and pinch, the same deliberate pinch as everywhere else, so you can go from game to game without the mouse.

**🎯 Choose your aim** shows, for each hand, every way the cursor can follow your hand (palm centre, index fingertip, virtual fingertip, fitted palm, adaptive) and every way the camera picture can become the screen (flat box, eye-anchored, eye-anchored with the nine-point fit, arm angle). For each one it gives your hit percentage from the rounds you played with it: in the pop game, the share of your pinches that popped a bubble; in the carry game, the share of carries that landed in the square. The one with the best pop percentage over at least 3 rounds is marked **★ Best for you so far**. Tap or pinch an option to use it from now on for that hand, or **🎲 Keep comparing** to go back to trying them in turn. Percentages from a few rounds can change a lot; about 10 rounds per option give a fair comparison. The end cards have the same button, and say when the option you just played has become your best.

## Distance

Once the camera is on, the first card's "Where do you sit from the camera?" answer is filled in for you from the gap between your eyes (the pupils are about 6.3 cm apart, so their gap in the picture tells how far away you are), with the distance shown beside it. You can still choose another answer yourself. During the games a small 📏 chip beside the light shows the distance; if you sit closer than about 40 cm or further than about 1.5 m for a few seconds it suggests moving, and if you sit more than 20% nearer or further than when you trained it says so (the eye-anchored camera models adjust by themselves; with the flat box a quick retrain fits the new distance). Each round keeps its typical distance, and the comparison page groups your rounds by distance next to the lighting.

## Lighting

Hand tracking depends on light, so both games measure it. Four times a second the camera picture is shrunk to a tiny 64 × 36 copy and a few numbers are worked out from it: how bright it is, how much contrast it has, how much is blown out or black, whether the edges are much brighter than the middle (a window or lamp behind you), how bright your hand is and how much it stands out from the background, the colour cast, and how much the brightness flickers. Only those numbers are kept; no picture is stored or sent.

During a game a small chip under the scoreboard says **Dark**, **Dim**, **Good**, **Bright** or **Backlit**. If it is Dark or Backlit for 3 seconds at the start of a round, the game says how to fix it, once. Each round also records how often your hand was lost (frames resting below the waist line don't count), how sure the tracker was that it was your hand, and how much the cursor wobbled while your hand was held still. The end card shows a Lighting line with these.

The private comparison (add `#compare` to the address) has a **Lighting and tracking** section for each hand: your rounds grouped by light, with how often the hand was lost, the wobble, the pop game's throughput and the carry game's placement for each group, and a straight-line fit of each of those on brightness and on how much your hand stands out. The feature and model trials allow for the light too, so a round that went better because the room was brighter isn't credited to a feature.

## Sharing results

At the end you can **share your results with the project**. It opens a GitHub issue pre-filled with the numbers (settings, measurements, your answers and ratings, browser type, screen size), and you press Submit yourself. If you tick "Include my last game rounds", the scores, counts and precision numbers of your last few pop and carry rounds go with it, so the project can see how the games' tuning works out in practice; the game end cards have a Share results button too. A bot checks the numbers, stores them in `data/results.jsonl`, recomputes `data/defaults.json` (the medians, overall and per steadiness answer), and closes the issue. The atlas loads those shared defaults as its starting settings; a personal profile always overrides them. The shared numbers include each hand's pinch thresholds in the atlas's 3D space, so the shared pinch defaults can form. Each share sends only game rounds played since your last share, unless you tick "Include rounds I've shared before"; the bot also skips any round it already has, and `scripts/dedupe.py` is a one-off tool that removes rounds stored more than once by earlier shares (run it by hand; `--dry-run` shows the counts first).

Please read [PRIVACY.md](PRIVACY.md) first. In short: the camera never leaves your computer; sharing is optional, public, and under your GitHub account; this is not a medical test.

## Files

- `index.html`: the training page. No build step; everything is in this one file. Hand tracking is Google's MediaPipe Hand Landmarker (loaded from jsDelivr; the model from storage.googleapis.com), run entirely in your browser.
- `scripts/collect.py`, `.github/workflows/collect.yml`: the collector bot.
- `data/results.jsonl`, `data/defaults.json`: shared results and the defaults built from them. `data/archive/` keeps the first results (before v0.21), which are not used.
